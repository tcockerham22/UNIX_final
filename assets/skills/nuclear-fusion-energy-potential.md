## Summary
This study utilizes the Linearized Dynamic New Earth 21 (LDNE21) model to investigate the introduction conditions and potential of nuclear fusion energy for the 21st century. The research focuses on determining the cost of electricity (COE) required to introduce fusion power within maximum construction speed constraints while targeting an atmospheric CO2 concentration of 550 ppmv by 2100. The model assumes the availability of sequestration technologies and innovative future technologies for CO2 reduction.

## Key Concepts & Important Facts
- **Model Framework**: The study uses a world energy/environment model with 10 regional allocations and 10-year time intervals, minimizing total energy systems cost under carbon emission constraints.
- **Cost Targets (COE)**: To introduce nuclear fusion within maximum construction speed limits:
  - **2050 Introduction**: Requires a COE of 66 mill/kWh.
  - **2060 Introduction**: Requires a COE of 92 mill/kWh.
  - **2070 Introduction**: Requires a COE of 106 mill/kWh.
- **Current Technology Viability**: Present tokamak-type nuclear fusion reactors (based on current cost estimates) are expected to become viable for introduction between 2060 and 2070.
- **Electricity Generation Share**: If growth is limited only by maximum construction speed, nuclear fusion will generate approximately **20% of total electricity** in the year 2100.
- **CO2 Mitigation Contribution**: Introducing fusion in 2050 contributes about **20%** of the total CO2 reduction amount achieved by 2100 compared to a business-as-usual scenario without fusion.
- **Regional Deployment Strategy**: Fusion is assumed to be introduced first in North America, West Europe, Japan, and the Former Soviet Union. Other regions (China, South & East Asia, Africa, Latin America) face a 20-year delay. The Middle East and Oceania are excluded due to abundant primary energy resources.
- **Construction Speed**: World total nuclear fusion electricity capacity increase is set at 100 GW/yr, divided proportionally to regional electricity demand increases.

## Procedures & Methodology
1. **Model Setup**: Define the LDNE21 model parameters including regional allocations and IPCC IS92a reference energy demand scenarios.
2. **Parameter Definition**: Input technological properties for fusion, including initial COE, annual reduction rates (2.3%/yr for first 25 years, then 0.25%/yr), plant availability improvements (up to 83%), and capital cost reductions (20% via learning).
3. **Constraint Application**: Apply atmospheric CO2 concentration constraints (550 ppmv by 2100) and Kyoto Protocol constraints for Annex-I countries.
4. **Simulation**: Run simulations varying the introduction year and COE to find the minimum COE required to satisfy maximum construction speed constraints.
5. **Analysis**: Calculate electricity generation fractions and CO2 reduction contributions relative to business-as-usual scenarios.

## Takeaways & Applications
- Nuclear fusion is confirmed as a viable candidate for both energy supply and CO2 mitigation technologies in the 21st century.
- For large-scale use, two main factors are desired: **cost competitiveness** (achieving lower COE) and **removal of capacity constraint factors** (increasing construction speed beyond current limits).
- Fusion can play a significant role in global decarbonization strategies, particularly if introduced earlier (around 2050) with aggressive cost reductions.

## Limitations & Notes
- The study assumes specific technological learning curves and plant availability improvements that may vary based on future R&D outcomes.
- The model excludes the Middle East and Oceania from fusion deployment due to resource abundance assumptions, which may not hold if energy demand shifts or resources deplete.
- The analysis relies on the LDNE21 model structure, which simplifies complex real-world geopolitical and economic variables.
